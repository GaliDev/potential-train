3. COMPREHENSIVE Q&A BANK (The Grill Session)
================================================================================

Delivery rule: answer in <=2 sentences, cite the mechanism, then stop. Whoever

A. TECHNICAL & ARCHITECTURE

Q1. How do the 5 judges interact -- and what happens when they disagree?
They don't talk to each other; they're independent specialists that fan out in
parallel and write to a shared state list via LangGraph's add reducer.
"Disagreement" is resolved deterministically in the aggregator -- a weighted
mean (correctness 0.35, faithfulness 0.30, completeness 0.20, coherence 0.15)
for quality, with safety as a separate hard gate. So a 5/5 on quality with a
failed safety verdict still fails overall -- by design, not by vote.

Q2. Why a deterministic aggregator instead of a 6th "meta-judge" LLM?
Reproducibility, cost, and auditability. The thing that decides pass/fail must
give the same answer every time and be explainable to a risk team -- an extra
LLM call adds latency, cost, and non-determinism for no measured accuracy gain
(our deterministic aggregate already hits kappa 0.97).

Q3. Latency and token cost with 5 calls -- sequential or concurrent?
The graph fans out so the five run in the same superstep; wall-clock is bounded
by the slowest judge, not the sum. On cost: five small open-model calls total
~$0.00014/item -- cheaper than a single GPT-4o call (~$0.008) -- because we
route each criterion to a cheap specialist instead of one expensive generalist.

Q4. Five models is operational overhead. Isn't one good model simpler?
We tested exactly that -- the single GPT-4o panel is our documented baseline.
The per-criterion open panel matches its agreement (kappa 0.97) at ~1/57th the
cost, and three of the five criteria share one model (Qwen3-14B), so it's
effectively three model endpoints, all behind one HF router. The config is a
30-line JSON map (config/judge_panel.json), not five services.

Q5. How do you control bias and variance in LLM-as-judge?
Three ways. Variance: open-model judges are temperature-pinned, prompted for
strict JSON, and verdicts parsed leniently with a deterministic fallback. Bias:
we MEASURED it -- our benchmark exposed that Claude and Grok are systematically
lenient (mean safety score 5.0, kappa collapses), which is exactly why we didn't
ship them. Calibration: every judge is validated against human gold labels and
must clear kappa>=0.6 before its scores drive any governance decision.

Q6. 76 gold items is small -- how do you know it generalizes?
Agreed, it's a POC gold set; that's why we report chance-corrected kappa and
Spearman, not just accuracy, and why we span three task families plus a clinical
set. The architecture treats the gold set as a plug-in -- adding a task family
is a new gold file + a registry entry, with no engine change (we did exactly
that for medical-RAG). Scaling the gold set is the first production roadmap item.

Q7. What if a judge model returns malformed output?
The open-model path prompts for strict JSON and runs through parse_verdict,
which has regex rescue and a deterministic fallback verdict -- in the latest
benchmark, 0 hard failures and only a handful of regex rescues across 380 calls.
OpenAI paths use structured outputs, so they're schema-valid by construction.

Q8. Why LangGraph and not just asyncio.gather over 5 calls?
For this panel alone you could; LangGraph earns its place because the same
primitive powers our runtime agents (retrieve-check-retry, draft-verify-refine
loops with conditional edges and state), and it gives us typed state, reducers,
and a single .invoke interface. PanelJudge is a drop-in for the baseline judge
precisely because the graph is hidden behind one method.

Q9. Is the safety gate a single point of failure?
It's intentionally conservative -- it can only fail an item, never rescue one,
so the worst case is a false-negative (over-blocking), which in a governance
product is the safe direction. Safety is also the criterion we watch most
closely in the benchmark (Qwen3-14B: kappa 0.87 on safety).

Q10. How is overall_pass actually computed? (if pressed on code)
overall_pass = aggregate_score >= PASS_THRESHOLD AND safety.passed, where
aggregate_score is the weighted mean over the four quality criteria present.
It's ~10 lines in agents/aggregator.py -- deliberately boring.

B. BUSINESS & PRODUCT

Q11. Why is this a moat versus a single-prompt "judge wrapper"?
A prompt wrapper gives you a score; it doesn't give you a defensible action. Our
value is the closed loop -- a judge we've proven against human labels (kappa
0.97) drives routing, earned autonomy, and a policy gate, all with an audit
trail. The moat is the accumulating, validated performance history per agent --
that data compounds and isn't reproducible by re-prompting GPT-4o.

Q12. Who actually pays for this, and why now?
Platform/ML-ops engineers and risk owners running multiple agents in production.
"Now" because the incumbent categories (LangSmith/Phoenix, OpenRouter/Portkey,
ServiceNow AI Control Tower, Agentforce) each own one pillar and none connect
measured competence to governed action -- we're an early mover in the forming
"agent workforce management" category.

Q13. Does it scale beyond a demo with SQLite?
SQLite is a zero-setup choice for the POC; storage sits behind a Protocol, so it
swaps for a managed multi-tenant store without touching the engine. The compute
that scales -- judging -- is already cheap (~$0.00014/item) and embarrassingly
parallel.

Q14. Your fleet KPIs miss target -- isn't that a failure?
The opposite -- those misses are injected on purpose (a rag_weak safety
incident, a sum_weak latency spike). A governance product with a perfectly
healthy fleet would demonstrate nothing. The KPI that matters, judge agreement
with humans, clears every target, and gating the weak agents lifts served
quality 65%->92%.

Q15. What's the realistic failure mode in production?
Gold-set drift -- if real traffic diverges from the labeled distribution, judge
agreement can decay silently. Mitigation is on the roadmap: periodic
re-benchmarking against fresh human labels and using our own drift detector on
the judge's agreement, not just the agents'.

Q16. Two more weeks and a budget -- what do you build?
Predictive governance: use accumulated history to demote/alert before an
incident instead of reacting; expand the gold set with active-learning sampling
on low-confidence items; and a managed store for multi-tenant pilots.
